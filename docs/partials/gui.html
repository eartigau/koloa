The GUI: koloa in the browser

koloa --gui opens koloa in a web page served by this machine: a star by its name, its archives gathered, your files of velocities, a quick FIP of what is plotted, folds and orbits, a transit looked for in TESS, the detailed report and a script of the analysis, one target after another or many at once. Every run is the command line the page shows, word for word, in a process of its own; nothing is done that the command line cannot do, and the page is a way to look before choosing what to run.

Each part of the page has its own page here, with screenshots and what every control does; the walk-throughs then follow real stars from start to end. The screenshots are of public data only: the archives were gathered without a DACE key, and the files are public HARPS velocities. Every (i) of the GUI opens the full explanation of its control; these pages say why, and what to look at.

The GUI

Starting it

# the page in your browser, served on 127.0.0.1 only (the next free port
#   from 8765); Ctrl-C stops it, and the runs it started
koloa --gui

# the same, in one word
koloanui

The server runs from the folder you start it in: relative paths (the archives folder, the report folder, your files) start there, and the page says where. It answers this machine only and runs nothing but koloa. After an update of koloa, start it again: the page and the server go together.

The page has four tabs. Analysis is one star: its name, its velocities, the quick FIP, the folds, a transit in TESS, the archives to gather and the detailed report. Remembered targets lists the results kept, each to be recalled as it was. Batch FIP runs the quick FIP of many files, one after the other, into a table. Survey starts from a sample of stars asked of SIMBAD, and makes their batch, here or for a server. New target, at the top, empties the page as a new session would (a run that still runs goes on); EN/FR switches the language of the page, and it is kept.

The GUI

The star

Its SIMBAD name: GJ 436, Ross 905, HD 69830, TOI-700 or TIC 307210830 all work. Resolve (or Enter) asks the CDS for its identifiers, position, TIC and Gaia DR3 numbers and aliases, then the NASA Exoplanet Archive and exoplanet.eu for its known planets, and TESS for its TOIs. The answer is kept on disk (in its archives folder once gathered), and the page reads it from there the next time.

The known planets are the NASA Exoplanet Archive's, each with its published solutions, and under each the values of exoplanet.eu, a second opinion: the two mostly agree, not always (another paper taken), and each has planets, or times of transit, the other lacks (a planet only exoplanet.eu has is listed as such). Both catalogues are kept on this machine once fetched; exoplanet.eu's comes in the background the first time (its server takes minutes).

The mass of the star is the archive's for a planet host, else a rough one from its spectral type (the table of Pecaut and Mamajek). Its radius and effective temperature are best guesses, in that order: the NASA Exoplanet Archive, the TESS Input Catalog (Gaia, and the relations of Mann et al. for the cool dwarfs), APERO's database, the same table by spectral type. The radius sets the depths of a transit (and the radius of a planet from one), the temperature the equilibrium temperature of a planet.

The star of a file, by its APERO name

Give a file of velocities with the name empty, and the page fills it: the file's OBJECT column (LBL keeps the header key of each spectrum), else its name (lbl_<OBJECT>_<TEMPLATE>.rdb), looked up in APERO's database of names exactly as APERO looks (the same cleaning of the name, its variants and aliases), which gives its SIMBAD name. The line under the name says how.

APERO's database is the astrometrics folder of its assets. Its YAML files come first in the tarball of APERO's assets server, so koloa reads a few megabytes of it and stops, with no APERO installed. The copy is made at the first lookup, again with koloa --refresh-apero or the page's button (Batch FIP tab).

The GUI

The velocities

Your files, one row each: an LBL .rdb (SPIRou, NIRPS, HARPS, ESPRESSO, HARPS-N), a csv with rjd, vrad and svrad (and indicators with their errors), or a csv of DACE. The instrument column is left empty when the file says it (the names of its reduced files, APERO's keys), or names it (NIRPS_LBL2 against NIRPS, two reductions of one instrument, each with its own offset). Below them, the archives folder, and the archives already gathered for the star with their number of points: tick those to plot, and the report uses them.

Which datasets are used

The archives of a star often hold the same spectra more than once (HARPS through the ESO pipeline on DACE and through SERVAL on VizieR, HIRES in three surveys), and old velocities too imprecise to constrain anything. Everything is gathered, kept on disk, plotted and listed; the table unticks what two rules leave out, and says why beside each dataset.

  • The releases of the same spectra: a spectrum that several datasets have is taken from the most precise of them. On the spectra two releases share, the star does the same in both, so their difference is noise alone, and the covariance of each with that difference is its own noise: no model of the star is needed (its strongest signals are taken out of both first, which only sharpens the comparison; a release binned by night is compared bin for bin). When the noises cannot be told apart, a dataset that names its spectrograph comes before a table that names none, then the latest release, then the one with the more spectra. A release left with nothing of its own is unticked; one with spectra the better release lacks keeps those (its points used of its points, in the table).
  • The datasets that constrain nothing: a line is fitted to the nightly means, an offset per dataset and one slope; a dataset is unticked when taking it away makes the error of the mean and that of the slope grow by less than 1 %, the weakest first. The error of a night holds what the star does about a line, the same for every instrument, so a few nights among hundreds weigh little whatever their precision.

Tick a dataset to ask it back: a release asked back is preferred to the others for the spectra they share, and the one used before it is unticked in its turn. The datasets of your own files are never left out. The report, the batch and the analysis script make the same choice (--exclude, --include; --no-rules for every dataset, each spectrum still used once).

Zero: each instrument about the solution shown (fitted offsets) or about its own median. The solution is one for every instrument, the trend and the signals; each instrument sits about it, the median of its velocities minus it zero over its own epochs, so that the instruments run on from one to the next. Colour by the instrument or the BERV (from LBL, DACE and CARMENES). The DACE copy of an instrument of your files (NIRPS_DACE: the same spectra through DACE's pipeline, less precise than LBL's) is left out the first time it is seen, and the note says so: one tick adds it back.

The GUI

Gathering the archives

What is known of a star, in one folder of its own (archives/GJ_436/): DACE (HARPS, ESPRESSO, NIRPS, CORALIE... with your key, what your account may see; without it, the public data), CARMENES DR1 (about 360 M dwarfs of the north, 2016 to 2020, corrected for the nightly zero points), the velocities published on VizieR, and every TESS sector at MAST (with the light curves of Kepler, K2 and CoRoT when the star lay in their fields). VizieR has a box per source: Keck HIRES (Teklu et al. 2025; Tal-Or et al. 2019), the California Legacy Survey (HIRES, the APF and the Lick Hamilton), the Lick Hamilton (Fischer et al. 2014), HARPS by SERVAL (Trifonov et al. 2020), and the tables of the star's papers in its SIMBAD bibliography.

The GUI

The quick FIP

A first look before the report: the FIP of exactly what the plot shows (the files, the archives ticked, the instruments kept), outlier-aware, in nightly means, with no GP. It starts on its own for one instrument; with several, untick those to leave out first, then compute it (Stop ends it). Two passes, as in the report: the errors of each instrument inflated to the noise of a fit without planets, a FIP; then to the noise of a fit with the signals found and the known planets (their variance is not noise), and the FIP again. About a minute for one instrument, longer with many; a bar shows the time left.

Click the periodogram, or type a period in Fold at P, to fold anywhere: the fold snaps to the dip of the FIP within a peak's width. Recompute the FIP after changing what is plotted; Remember the result keeps all of it.

The GUI

The folds

The nightly means folded at a numbered peak: a sinusoid fitted with an offset per instrument and the trend, each night weighted by its probability of being valid (from the FIP: an outlier hardly counts), phase 0 at the conjunction. Its period is refined within the peak, the other peaks fitted with it there (the grid point of the FIP is a fraction of a peak off: over a long series, enough for a sinusoid to drift in phase from one end to the other). Its line gives P with its error, K and the rms; with the mass of the star, the minimum mass in Earth, Neptune and Jupiter masses (its errors from draws of K, P and the mass, one ± when its two sides are alike), and the equilibrium temperature of the planet (for a Bond albedo of 0 and of 0.3, from the period, the mass, the radius and the temperature of the star).

The buttons beyond the peaks fold at the published orbit of each known planet (★, its solution and envelope drawn, whatever the FIP says) and at the transit ephemeris of each transiting planet or TOI (◐: phase 0 at the transit, the ephemeris carried to the velocities with its error, and the conjunction the velocities put on their own set against it).

Tick a signal (the box before its button) to take it out of the velocities: its sinusoid, or its Keplerian orbit when that is the model shown as it is ticked (not its offsets and trend, which the FIP fits again). Several can be ticked: each fold is then fitted on the velocities without the other ticked signals, the solution on the time series is their sum, and the FIP of what is left is computed after each subtraction, in the order ticked, and drawn over the FIP of the series in the colour of its tick. A second planet the first one hid shows up, or the activity, or nothing; a click on a peak of the last one folds there, and a tick goes on; untick to put a signal back. Tick the Keplerian of an eccentric orbit rather than its sinusoid: a sinusoid leaves the orbit's harmonic at half the period, a peak of its own in what is left.

The GUI

A transit in TESS

A transit found would make a weak velocity signal strong. Under the folds, each peak with a FIP below 10 % is looked for in TESS, about the conjunction of its fold (where a transiting planet transits), and so is each transiting planet and TOI of the star at its ephemeris. The light curve is the star's archives', else every sector at MAST. A star TESS has not observed yet has none, and the panel says so (its sectors leave gaps: GJ 1214, for one, is in none so far), with the sectors to come when tess-point is installed.

Kepler, K2 and CoRoT are searched too when the star lay in their fields: the long cadence of Kepler, the K2SFF light curves of K2 where there are some, the light curves of CoRoT kept at the CDS. The first mission with a plausible transit is the one shown, and a button for each mission that has the star searches its light curve.

  • The light curve: split into stretches (a sector, at its gaps of more than 0.2 day), the first 0.25 day after each gap left out (the ramp of TESS as it starts again), each stretch high-passed on its own (a running median three times the expected transit, a robust line near its ends), flares clipped.
  • The search: boxes of half, one and two times the duration of a central transit, within 3 sigma of the expected time carried to the epochs of TESS (the error of a conjunction from a sinusoid includes the offset an eccentric orbit gives it), or over the whole phase. When the error of the period would smear the transits across sectors years apart, the period itself is scanned, coarse then fine, and the light curve folded at the best.
  • The depth: a box fitted by least squares to the medians of the folded light curve in fine bins (the deepest box of the search follows the deepest points; the fit's depth is the transit's mean level), and the radius of the planet from it.
  • Plausible: 7 sigma or more, in 2 transits or more, still 3.5 sigma without the deepest (one event is not a transit seen twice), and beyond the best box at periods where there is nothing, with a chance below 1 % (an active star, or the systematics of a light curve, make dips of their own at any period). The transits of the star's other known planets are left out first.

The search was tested on LHS 1140 b and c, TOI-700 b, c, d and e and GJ 436 b (found at their published radii, from their ephemerides, from a conjunction as the velocities give it and over the whole phase) and at periods where there is nothing (none found), and in the other missions on Kepler-21 b (Kepler), K2-18 b and K2-3 b, c and d (K2) and CoRoT-7 b (CoRoT). A star as active as AU Mic makes dips as deep as its planets at any period, and the panel says so rather than claim a transit.

The GUI

The detailed report and the analysis script

The card of the report sets its options (the signals of the FIP and its sweeps, the periods to search, a GP of the activity in the FIP, by band or an SHO at the rotation, the TOIs to fit with their transits, the instruments to leave out, the trend, every exposure or nightly means, the archives, a detection map, an MCMC of the orbits) and shows the command line they make; Make the report runs it, and its steps and log show in Runs, its PDF at the end.

Analysis script writes a kit to run, and change, the analysis yourself, offline: a .tar.gz with analysis.py, the analysis step by step calling koloa's routines (the velocities read and put together, a fit of their noise, the FIP in two passes, the Keplerian orbits of the signals with their errors and minimum masses, the acceleration of the star, the folds, the FIP of the residuals), every step explained at length in its comments in the language of the page and its figures saved as PDF; your files; the archives gathered for the star; and star.yaml, what SIMBAD, the archive and TESS say of the star. Its settings are those of the card: a starting point for a student, or for a change koloa does not make.

The PDF button of the plot writes the quick look as it is shown (the series in its ranges, the quick FIP, the folds, the transit), with a page of numbers.

The GUI

Remembered targets

Remember the result keeps the page as it is (the star, the files, the archives ticked, the instruments left out, the options of the report), the quick FIP and its folds, and a copy of the velocities and of the archives, with a note of your own. The Remembered targets tab lists them; Recall puts one back in the Analysis tab as it was (the original files while they have not changed, else their copies).

The GUI

The batch FIP

The quick FIP of many files, one after the other: add them (several in the dialog, or every file of a folder that matches a pattern, or one of several patterns separated by spaces) and run. Each file is on its own, or, with every archive ticked, with every archive of its star (DACE, CARMENES DR1, VizieR; gathered once into the archives folder, then read from there). The star of each file is found by its APERO name.

Click a header to sort, CSV to save it (with the APERO and SIMBAD names, the planets, the instruments and the transit), Open to take a file to the Analysis tab with its quick FIP, its star and its archives. The table fits the window; Stop ends the batch.

The GUI

A survey, and its batch on a server

The Survey tab starts from constraints, not from a star: a range of spectral types and a distance (every M dwarf within 15 pc, say), between two declinations, brighter than a V if you give one. Ask SIMBAD: the stars come back as a table, the nearest first, each under its GJ name when it has one, with its type, its distance from its parallax, its V and its rotation period when SIMBAD lists one (that of the latest paper, the others and their references under the cursor; that of CARMENES DR1 is added by the cross-match). A system and its components are objects of their own in SIMBAD: a mark says when another object of the sample is within 5 arcsec.

  • Cross-match with the archives tells which stars have velocities, without gathering them: CARMENES DR1 and the surveys on VizieR by the star's position in their lists, and DACE asked star by star, six at a time (a few seconds a star). What DACE answers is kept in the archives folder, where the gathering reads it back. Its summary counts the stars with velocities and, spectrograph by spectrograph (the eras of an instrument and its releases put together: HARPS on DACE and in the RVBank is one line), the stars, the velocities counted and the archives that have them. The table says the same of each star; tick some data, and the stars that have nothing (no velocity in an archive, no file of yours) are no longer shown.
  • Your files: folders of LBL files of SPIRou, of NIRPS... The star of each file is its OBJECT column, else its name, through APERO's names, and the file goes with the star of the sample that has that name. The files of stars that are not in the sample are counted.
  • Tick the stars to run: those with data, those with files, all, none; a click on a header sorts the table.

The batch of the stars ticked is the quick FIP of each, with its files and every archive of it, its datasets chosen by the rules (The velocities). Run it here sends it to the Batch FIP tab. Or pack it for another machine, a server or a cluster: one folder, and its .tar.gz, with everything the batch needs and no path of this machine in it.

m_dwarfs_demo/
  README.txt      # how to run it there, for a person or for a Claude session on that machine
  run_batch.py    # the script; its first setting is ROOT, where this folder is there
  submit.sh       # the same as a SLURM job array, a star per task
  targets.json    # the stars, and the files of each
  files/          # the files of velocities of each star
  archives/       # its archives, gathered before packing: no network needed there
  cache/          # what koloa fetched once (the NASA Exoplanet Archive, the lists of the surveys)
  koloa_src/      # koloa as it was when the batch was packed: nothing to install there
  results/        # made by the batch: a folder per star, and table.csv

# there
tar xzf m_dwarfs_demo.tar.gz
cd m_dwarfs_demo
python run_batch.py --root "$PWD" --check     # nothing computed: is everything there?
nohup python -u run_batch.py --root "$PWD" > run.log 2>&1 &
python run_batch.py --root "$PWD" --check     # again: where is each star?
python run_batch.py --root "$PWD" --stop      # a batch stopped goes on where it was

Each star done is kept as it goes (its line of the table, its FIP as the page draws it, a PDF of its quick look), and results/table.csv gathers them. Brought back, the results of a batch folder open in the page as a batch: its table, and each star in the Analysis tab with its FIP as it was computed.

Running it there

The tar is copied and run outside koloa, by you or by a Claude session on the machine that runs it. Its README.txt says the whole of it, with the names and the paths of this batch in its commands: how to copy it, what it needs (Python 3.9 or later with numpy, scipy and matplotlib; nothing is installed and nothing is asked of the network), how to start it and leave it running, a job array on a cluster, how long stars took, how to bring results/ back, what each column of the table is, what to do when something goes wrong, and the list of its stars with what was packed of each. A section of it is written for an agent: the steps in order, what each must answer before the next, and what not to do (install, fetch, delete the results, start the batch twice, kill processes on a guess, copy the data elsewhere).

Two options of the script are made for that. --check computes nothing: it says which Python it is and what it has, the cores of the machine, and of each star whether it is done (its best peak), failed (why), being computed (how far its FIP is, and the time left) or to do; its last line is the state of the batch, ready, running, done, or not ready with what is missing. --stop ends the processes of this batch on this machine, with the chains of their FIPs, and no other; the stars done are kept.

The GUI

GJ 436 from its name

No file at all: the star's name, its public archives, and what the GUI makes of them, step by step. The numbers below are those of the run that took the screenshots of these pages.

  1. Type GJ 436 and Resolve: the star card (The star).
  2. Gather the archives (here with no DACE key: the public data), then tick them all under the velocities and Plot (The velocities). The table unticks the releases of spectra that another dataset has more precisely, and the datasets that constrain nothing.
  3. Compute the quick FIP, untick an instrument first if it should not count (The quick FIP).
  4. Look at the fold of peak #1, coloured by date, as a Keplerian, on the series (The folds).
  5. Read the TESS panel under it (A transit in TESS).
  6. Tick peak #1 with the Keplerian chosen: its orbit is taken out, and the FIP of what is left is drawn over the first (The folds).
  7. Remember the result; make the report, or the analysis script, from the same card (The report and the script).

The GUI

Five stars in one batch

Five files of public HARPS velocities, one per star, each with its OBJECT column, run together in the Batch FIP tab, each file on its own: the star of each found by its APERO name, its quick FIP, and a transit looked for in its space photometry where the FIP is below 10 %. With every archive ticked, the archives of each star are gathered and put with its file first: many more velocities, and, with a dozen datasets a star, a far longer run.

The GUI

A file of your own, and its archives

The everyday case: an LBL file of SPIRou or NIRPS, its star, and what the archives add.

  1. Pick the file (Browse...) with the name empty: the star is filled from its OBJECT column (or its file name) through APERO's names, and resolved (The star).
  2. Gather the archives of the star, tick those to add, and Plot. An instrument the file has too (NIRPS in the file and on DACE) is the same spectra by another pipeline: the archive's is named apart (NIRPS_DACE, its own offset), its exposures within a minute of the file's left out, and NIRPS_DACE itself is left out by default; tick it to compare.
  3. Several files go in one analysis, a row each: SPIRou and NIRPS, or two reductions of one instrument named apart in the instrument column.
  4. Then as for GJ 436: the quick FIP, the folds, a transit in TESS, and the report with the same settings.
